What is corporate finance?

Ask ten people what finance is and nine will describe the stock market — the spectator sport, not the game. This long read is the game: Prof. Tantri's corporate finance course rebuilt as an essay you can play with. The same examples, the same numbers, the same traps he sets in class — and seven machines to break with your own hands. By the end you will know what a CFO actually decides all day, which, conveniently, is the stuff you can't just Google.

Why this exists

Marketing, ops, hiring — every function rolls up to one KPI, and the KPI is money. Finance is the science of that KPI. Macro is the art around it — the weather the machine runs in. Chapter 1What finance actually does

Start with the misconception. "Finance" in most heads means trading screens, red and green, a man on TV shouting a target price. That is a thin slice of a much bigger machine, and not the important slice. Here is the whole machine, as the professor lays it out in Session 1.

Finance does two big jobs. First, it moves resources from people who have them to people who can use them — from savers to investors. And note the vocabulary, because he is particular about it: an investor here is someone who invests in a real project — a factory, a fleet, a kitchen — not someone passively holding shares. Money itself is just the token that represents the resources. On its own it has no value. You cannot eat a ₹500 note; you can eat what it moves.

Second, finance distributes risk and reward between those two parties. The menu runs from debt at one end — the saver is promised a fixed return, the investor eats the surprises — to equity at the other, where the saver signs up for the surprises in exchange for the upside. In between sit insurance-like contracts, where an intermediary carries risk for a fee. Every security you will ever meet is some point on this line: who bears how much of the uncertainty, for what compensation.

Around these two jobs, three supporting functions. Information generation: someone has to collect and process the numbers so decisions aren't made blind — and having one intermediary monitor a borrower instead of a thousand small savers each doing it badly saves an enormous duplication of effort. Valuation: putting a number on an asset — the heart of the professor's course, and of this essay. And liquidity creation, which he flags, in his own words, as in his view the most important function finance performs. It deserves a paragraph, because it is genuinely clever.

Here is the problem liquidity creation solves. You, a saver, don't know when you will suddenly need your money — a wedding, a hospital, a visa deadline. Projects, meanwhile, are illiquid: half-built factories return nothing if abandoned. If every saver could yank money out of real projects on demand, every panic would force early liquidation and society would burn value for nothing. A bank solves this with actuarial cunning: it estimates what fraction of depositors will actually need cash at any time, keeps that much liquid, and locks the rest into long, illiquid, productive projects. It converts illiquid assets into liquid liabilities. Magic — until the day every depositor shows up at once, which is why bank runs are the recurring nightmare of financial history, and why the daily money-history card on this site keeps returning to them.

Now narrow the lens. Households manage budgets and retirement — household finance. Governments tax, spend and borrow — public finance. Firms — corporate finance, our subject. And within a firm, the professor's Session 1 list is blunt: the financial manager makes exactly three kinds of decisions. Investing decisions — which projects to do; this is capital budgeting, and the test is whether a project clears the hurdle of expected return. Financing decisions — what mix of debt and equity pays for it all, at the lowest cost of capital. Dividend decisions — how much profit goes back to shareholders versus staying in the business, and the rule is one line: reinvest if the marginal return on investment beats what shareholders could earn elsewhere; pay out if it doesn't.

However, start-ups are rarely incorporated as LLPs. Partners are considered part of the management, so it is easy for courts to lift the veil and make the liability unlimited at the drop of a hat. VCs and other investors dread this possibility."

"The point is, there are trade-offs. If you want someone passionate about teaching, the person will have the same passion for setting the exams. Whenever someone says something offers the best of both worlds, treat it with caution and analyse."

Three decisions. The rest of this essay is those three decisions taken seriously — which mostly means learning to see costs and values that never appear in an accountant's ledger. That is Chapter 2, and it starts with a trap that catches practicing managers, not just students.

Chapter 2The cost that never appears in the books

Here is a decision that looks trivial and isn't. Two projects on your desk, A and B. Project A: revenue 100, materials 30, wages 50 — profit 20. Project B: revenue 120, materials 30, wages 50 — profit 40. Every accounting system in the world, and most managers, say B. Forty beats twenty.

One detail. B needs your skilled workers, and they are currently running project C, which earns 50 a year. There is a shortage of skilled labour; nobody else can be hired. So choosing B means shutting C. Now redo the sum. B's true cost includes the 50 of profit that C stops earning — economists call it the opportunity cost, and it appears in no ledger. B's economic profit is 120 − 30 − 50 − 50 = minus 10. The "obviously better" project destroys value, and — as the notes put it flatly — the share price will decline if the manager goes with B on accounting logic.

The principle: the cost of doing anything is not what you pay out of pocket. It is out-of-pocket plus everything you gave up. Sounds obvious said aloud. Now watch how fast it stops being obvious — same projects, three situations:

Work through the third scenario, because it offends common sense most. C is shut, but company policy or labour law means the workers stay on payroll — their 50 of wages flows out whether they work or not. A new pair of projects arrives. B now shows an accounting loss of 10, and A a profit of 5. Yet B is the right answer: the wages are spent anyway — they are sunk — so B's real out-of-pocket labour cost is zero, and its economic profit is 40 against A's 5 or 20. Choose the project with the negative accounting profit, and the share price rises. If that sentence feels wrong in your stomach, good — that feeling is exactly what this chapter is for.

The mirror image of opportunity cost is sunk cost — money already spent, recoverable by no decision you can make now. The notes give the manager's version: you bought project A for 120 and project B for 30, years ago. Today you must raise 100 by selling one. A buyer offers 100 for either. Your own internal valuation says A is worth 80 and B is worth 120. Sell which? Obviously A — someone is offering 100 for a thing worth 80, take his money and run. What do managers actually do? They sell B. Selling A would mean "booking a loss" against the 120 they paid — a number that has no bearing on anything except their ego. The purchase price is gone; it is sunk; the only question is what each asset is worth versus what's offered today. Retail investors run the identical bug: clinging to losing stocks waiting to "recover their price," selling winners too early. The literature calls it the disposition effect. The professor calls it, correctly, a bias you should train out of yourself this term, because the market charges tuition forever.The evidence is fresh: a 2025 Journal of Finance paper (Guenzel, "In Too Deep") shows CEOs' decisions to divest a unit depend heavily on the price paid for it years ago — irrelevant to value now — and the effect vanishes when the divesting CEO is not the one who acquired it. The bias travels with the person, not the asset. The professor's advice to future consultants when he shared it in the group: "look for instances where organizations use sunk costs in decision making. This could be a good area of improvement."

So: the first discipline of finance is that decisions look only forward. Costs are what you give up from here; history is for accountants and sentimentalists. But "what you give up from here" raises the real question. When someone hands a firm money for a year, what exactly do they give up — and what is the fair charge for it? That number has a name — the cost of capital — and building it from scratch is the next chapter. It is behind the login, along with the machines for NPV, the IRR trap, leverage and payouts. Chapter 3The price of time and risk

Before a firm can judge any project, it needs to know what its money costs. Not roughly — actually. The professor builds this number from the ground up, and the ground floor is a bag of rice.

The first thing he posted after the course ended — on risk and return

"You hear this adage that risk and return go together. Not always true — high risk and low return exist too: uninformed investors trading stocks, or playing Russian roulette for ₹10,000. (Did you say taking my course also?)"

"You will never get to decide knowing the actual outcomes — except in cooked-up case studies. Evaluate every decision on the risks and expected returns at the time it was taken. From this view, the US government making 7% bailing out banks was no great achievement given the risk of losing everything; Warren Buffett made 39% on the same investments. And judge your future teams this way: just because a division made money does not make it successful — next time they may blow up the organization."

Imagine a world with no money, no inflation, no risk. You hold 10 kg of rice. I ask to borrow 1 kg today, returned tomorrow. Exactly 1 kg back? You refuse — parting with rice today means postponing consumption you actually value. So I offer 1.2 kg tomorrow, and now you consider it. Ask for your second kg and you will demand still more — say 1.4 — because each further kilo you give up costs you dearer consumption. That schedule — how much extra tomorrow-rice society demands per unit of today-rice — is the real rate of interest. It exists before money, before banks, before Excel. It is the price of impatience, and it is set by savers' preferences, not by any committee in Mumbai. Add expected inflation on top — compensation for the rupee buying fewer dosas next year — and you have the risk-free rate, roughly what a government bond pays.

Now add the third floor: risk. Follow the notes' example. You can deposit at SBI at 10%, near risk-free. A firm asks you to lend instead. You know that, among firms like this one, about 10% default and pay back nothing. What do you charge? Not 10% — at 10% you lose money in expectation. You need the survivors to cover the corpses. Charge r such that 90% of borrowers paying (1+r) returns what SBI would have paid: r = 22.22%. Check it: lend 100 to each of many such firms, get back 0.9 × 122.22 ≈ 110 — exactly the deposit outcome. That extra 12.22 points is not profit and not a risk premium. It is bookkeeping for the defaults. This is what a perfectly calm, risk-neutral lender must charge merely to break even. Price it yourself:

Two refinements turn that machine into the real world. First: actual lenders are not calm. The 10% default rate is an average; this year could be 30%. Humans dislike that uncertainty and charge extra for bearing it — that extra, above the arithmetic, is the true risk premium, and it swells with both the uncertainty and the lender's risk-aversion. In a crisis both swell together, which is why credit gets brutally expensive precisely when firms are desperate — the machine above explains spreads on a normal Tuesday, and the premium explains 2008 and IL&FS-autumn 2018.

Second, a subtlety worth an exam question: whose risk-aversion sets the price? Not the average lender's — the marginal one's. If you must sell two identical bonds and your friendly first buyer accepts 7% while the last buyer you need demands 10%, both bonds pay 10%. You cannot price-discriminate; the reluctant last lender the market still needs sets the price for everyone. Prices live at the margin. Remember this the next time a promoter complains that "the market" misprices his bonds — the market is just the last person he needed to convince.

Equity works on the same skeleton with a wilder payoff: no promised interest, last claim on everything, so shareholders demand the risk-free rate plus a fatter premium for a bumpier ride. Blend the two — equity's expected return weighted by equity's share of firm value, debt's by debt's — and you get the firm's overall price of money: the weighted average cost of capital, WACC. In practice you'll estimate the debt piece from credit ratings or interest-coverage ratios, and the equity piece from models like CAPM; the course does this properly with betas. For this essay you need only the meaning: WACC is the return the firm must beat for a project to be worth doing — the hurdle. Which raises the obvious question: beat it how, measured on what? Chapter 4, and it is the best chapter.

Chapter 4NPV — the only number the share price obeys

Session 6 of the course opens the toolbox for project selection, and the professor starts with the economically soundest tool: net present value. The formula is one line — the present value of cash inflows minus the present value of outflows — and the example in the notes is deliberately small. A project costs 100 today and pays 12, 11, and 110 at the ends of years one, two and three. The cost of capital is 10%.

Discount each flow to today: 12/1.1 + 11/1.1² + 110/1.1³ = 102.645. Subtract the 100. NPV = 2.645. The rule: with no resource constraints, take every positive-NPV project; if projects are mutually exclusive, take the highest positive one. So far, mechanical. Now the two ideas that make this chapter matter.

First idea. Why exactly 2.645 — what does the number mean? The notes prove it with what he calls finance profit: each year, charge the project the cost of capital on the invested 100 — that's 10 a year, the opportunity cost of the money — and see what's left. Year one: 12 − 10 = 2, worth 1.818 today. Year two: 11 − 10 = 1, worth 0.826. Year three: 110 pays back the 100 plus its final 10 of rent — nothing left. Total: 2.645. The NPV is the project's entire profit after paying money its full rent. Which is why, in an efficient market, the share price should jump by exactly the NPV the moment the project is announced — investor A holding a 100 stake will not sell to investor B for less than 102.645 once the project is public. The professor is honest about the friction: in practice investors don't fully trust managements' projections, so prices move directionally rather than exactly. But the limiting logic is the point: NPV is not a score. It is the amount of shareholder wealth the decision creates, and the market reprices it immediately. (And the new buyer at 102.645 still earns his 10% from there — run the year-one arithmetic and see.)

Second idea — the hidden assumption, and this is where the machine gets interesting. Baked silently into that 2.645 is the premise that the 12 and 11 arriving in years one and two get reinvested at 10% until the end. Park them in a bank at 6% instead and the true value of the inflows drops to 101.535 — NPV falls to 1.535. The notes push further: tweak year one's flow from 12 to 10 and the project is positive under the 10% reinvestment assumption and negative under 6%. Same project, same headline projections, opposite verdicts — hinging on an assumption nobody states in the board meeting. Break it yourself:

Every valuation you will ever see carries assumptions like that one, stated or not. The course's whole method is to drag them into the light. And nowhere do they hide better than inside finance's most beloved single number: the internal rate of return.

IRR is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero — "the project's return," as everyone sloppily says. For our cash flows Excel reports 11.07%, and the selection rule mirrors NPV's: take projects whose IRR beats the cost of capital. Being a ratio, it feels comparable and portable, which is why practitioners loved it for decades. But run the analogy from the NPV discussion — the professor sets this as an exercise before revealing it — and you can guess the flaw: IRR silently assumes the interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself. The 12 and the 11 are presumed to compound at 11.07% — a rate that exists nowhere except inside the formula. Park them at a real bank at 6% and the true annualised return is 10.56%, not 11.07%. Now imagine your hurdle is 10.75% — the naive IRR says take it, the truth says it destroys value:

One honest exception, from the notes: if a project has no interim cash flows — one outflow now, one inflow at the end — there is nothing to reinvest, and IRR is exactly what it claims to be. Rare in practice. Everywhere else, treat a quoted IRR the way you treat a matrimonial-ad height: directionally informative, self-reported, and rounded up. When NPV and IRR disagree on mutually exclusive projects, trust NPV — it is denominated in the only unit that matters, rupees of shareholder wealth, and its reinvestment assumption (the cost of capital) is at least a rate that exists. The balance in industry has been shifting to NPV; now you know why.

Chapter 5Capital structure — the pizza does not grow

On to the financing decision: what mix of debt and equity should pay for all this? Here the course sets a trap on purpose. Debt costs 5%, equity costs 15% — so pile on the cheap debt and the average cost falls, no? The Session 8 notes even run the naive calculation for you: a firm earning 150 in a good year and 80 in a bad year, valued all-equity at 100, appears to be worth 104.35 the moment you fund 50 of it with debt. Free value, from a refinancing. If that were true, every CFO's job would be one phone call.

The flaw: the calculation discounted the equity holder's now-riskier cash flows at the same old 15%. Look at what debt did to the equity: in the good year the shareholder gets 97.5, in the bad year 27.5 — the swings are violently wider than 150/80. Leverage concentrated all the risk onto a smaller equity slice, and risk is never free. Price the levered equity at the return that risk actually commands and the "extra" 4.35 evaporates. Firm value: exactly what it was. This is the Modigliani–Miller theorem, and the professor's one-line version is the right one: debt is cheaper, but having debt makes equity costlier, and the two cancel. Slicing the pizza differently does not create pizza. The value of a firm comes from its projects — the left side of the balance sheet — not from financial origami on the right side.

One September evening on the group, someone was surprised equity can go negative

"Equity can become negative. Why are people surprised? On a serious note — this is where economics and accounting diverge. For an economist, negative equity is nonsense, because liability is limited (you can't touch anyone's kidney): you cannot ask equity investors to bring money from home. An economist records a debt write-down with equity at zero — and fails an accounting exam. An accountant shows negative equity with debt intact — and fails an economics exam."

"Negative debt-to-equity? A ratio can't be negative unless debt or equity is. Equity takes the first hit — only after it is entirely wiped out can you start touching debt. So a negative ratio means negative equity."

MM holds under stated assumptions: no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, and individuals able to borrow on the same terms as firms (so any investor could home-brew his own leverage and arbitrage away any premium). The assumptions are the syllabus for the rest of the chapter — relax them one at a time and watch where real value appears and disappears. Relax tax first: interest is deductible in India as nearly everywhere, so every rupee of interest shields profit from tax. In the notes' example the shield is small — the equity holder gets exactly 0.5 more in each state — but it is free and riskless, and it scales with debt. Score one real point for leverage. Then relax bankruptcy — and this is where the machine below earns its place. Borrow enough that a bad year can't cover the repayment, and something subtle happens first: nothing. The lender, seeing default coming in the bad state, simply charges more upfront — in the notes' numbers, 23.75% instead of 5% — so that he still earns 5% in expectation. Default alone just reshuffles who gets paid; the firm is still worth 86.96. Value is destroyed only when bankruptcy has real costs — customers flee a wobbling firm, lawyers bill, assets sell in a hurry — modelled in the notes as the bad-state cash flow dropping from 50 to 40. Assemble the pieces and you get the working formula of modern capital structure: Vlevered = Vunlevered + PV(tax shield) − PV(bankruptcy costs). The optimal amount of debt is a trade-off between a tax benefit that grows steadily and a distress cost that grows explosively. Steady, predictable businesses — toll roads, utilities — can push far along the curve; volatile ones cannot. India's 2010s corporate-debt hangover, from the power-and-steel promoters to IL&FS, was a decade-long demonstration of firms mispricing the right-hand term. The equity holder bears the bankruptcy cost in advance, through the lender's rate — the notes even make you verify it two ways.

But debt does one more thing MM never priced: it changes how people behave. Session 10 ends on two behavioural diseases of the indebted, and they explain more Indian corporate news than any valuation model. The first is risk shifting — also answering to gambling for resurrection, overinvestment, asset substitution; the professor warns any of these may appear in the exam. The setup: you owe 100, the cash flow is 90, your equity is worth zero. A terrible bet appears — stake 20 for a 10% chance of doubling. For the firm it is ruinous in expectation. For you it is free money: your downside is already zero, so any lottery ticket bought with the lender's recovery is pure upside. Run it:

The second disease is the mirror image: debt overhang, or underinvestment. Same firm — debt of 100 due, cash flow of 90 fenced off by covenants. Now a genuinely good opportunity appears: put in 10 fresh, get 15 for sure — riskless 50%. The firm should obviously do it. But the equity holder must supply the 10, and of the extra 15, the first 10 goes to topping up the lender's recovery. The shareholder puts in 10 and gets back 5. So he refuses, and a positive-NPV project dies. Too little debt tempts nobody; too much debt poisons even good ideas. (Two students asked the sharp follow-up — why doesn't the bank fund the sure thing? Because in the real world no project is riskless, and lending fresh money to a defaulting borrower is restructuring, which triggers provisioning. The bank's own accounting stops it.)

These two diseases matter enough that the professor was still drilling them months after the course ended — announcing one morning on the group, apropos of campus security: "Will turn the main gate into Strait of Hormuz today. Hope you guys remember the difference between risk shifting and debt overhang." You have the machines above; you have no excuse.

Balance the ledger before moving on: debt also disciplines. A manager with fat free cash and no interest bill is a manager one flattering investment banker away from an empire-building acquisition; a manager with an EMI thinks twice. Covenants, for all their menace, are how lenders bottle that discipline. Debt is neither cheap fuel nor poison — it is a governance instrument with a tax subsidy and a failure mode. Which brings us, finally, to the decision everyone watches and few understand: what to do with the profits.

Chapter 6The payout — and what firms actually do

The profits are in. Keep them or return them? The rule from Session 1 is a single comparison: reinvest if the marginal return on investment beats the shareholders' expected return; pay out if it doesn't. If the company can compound the rupee better than its owners can elsewhere, keep it working. If it can't, handing it back isn't weakness — it's the only honest move, because retaining cash you can't deploy above the cost of capital destroys value with a straight face. Growth companies with rich project pipelines rightly retain; mature businesses with few high-return openings rightly distribute. The hard part isn't the rule; it's the CEO admitting which kind of company he runs.

Cash comes back through two pipes. Dividends — money lands in every account, taxed since 2020 as the shareholder's income (an earlier, stranger regime taxed the company instead, and payout fashion bent around the tax code, as it always does). Or buybacks — the company purchases its own shares. Buybacks come bundled with a party trick you should learn to see through: retiring shares shrinks the denominator, so earnings per share rises even when earnings don't. Now, the closing move the professor makes in the notes, and it may be the most useful thing in the whole course: what do firms actually do? Theory says invest in everything above the cost of capital. Practice keeps a buffer. When Tata Steel's CFO was asked about return hurdles on an earnings call, the answer — quoted in the notes — was that WACC hurdles run above 12% in India and 9–10% in Europe, while the return on invested capital they aim for "is always at about 15%." A three-point cushion, held deliberately. A 2025 study in the American Economic Review (Gormsen and Huber) finds this is the norm: firms' hurdle rates sit stubbornly above their perceived cost of capital, and when the cost of capital falls, hurdle rates barely move — full transmission takes something like twelve years. Pause on what that does to monetary policy: the RBI cuts rates so that cheaper capital spurs investment, banks eventually pass it on — and then firms don't lower their hurdles anyway. The lever moves; the machine barely does. This is over and above the slow bank transmission the RBI already loses sleep about — file that away for the macro sessions.

Financing shows the same theory-practice gap: firms don't run at the textbook-optimal debt mix; they carry less and pay fees for standby credit lines, buying financial flexibility — the ability to move in a bad year — at the cost of a not-quite-minimal WACC. None of this means the theory is wrong; the professor's disclaimer is worth keeping verbatim in spirit: adjustments happen as theory predicts, just slowly, because real equipment, real customers and real fears move slower than spreadsheets. The models tell you where gravity points. Practice tells you about the friction on the way down.

So: what is corporate finance? Three decisions, priced honestly.

Decide what to buy — counting opportunity costs the ledger hides, ignoring costs already sunk, and scoring projects by the one number the share price obeys (Chapters 2 and 4). Decide whose money to use — knowing the lender's arithmetic, why the pizza doesn't grow, and how debt bends behaviour (Chapters 3 and 5). Decide what to return — by the marginal-return rule, seeing through the EPS theatre (Chapter 6). The course's own parting advice compresses it further: whenever anyone shows you a valuation, probe the assumptions — the reinvestment rate hiding in the NPV, the hurdle sitting quietly above the cost of capital, the bad state nobody modelled.

What can you do with this tomorrow? Read one annual report the way this essay reads projects: find the capex, ask what hurdle it cleared and whether interim cash could really compound there; check whether debt matches the steadiness of the cash flows; and when EPS grows, check how much came from the numerator. Twenty minutes a quarter. It is more than most professionals do.

And when your brother-in-law from IIM-A quotes a stock's IRR at the next family dinner, ask him — gently — what reinvestment rate it assumes. Then enjoy your dinner in peace.
